# Klub výpočetní techniky Královo Pole

The **Klub výpočetní techniky Královo Pole** (KVT Královo Pole) stands as a
remarkable testament to the ingenuity and determination of amateur computing
enthusiasts in socialist Czechoslovakia. Founded in 1974 as a hobby section of
the local ROH culture house in the Královo Pole district of Brno, the club
would go on to occupy a unique and enduring place in the rich tapestry of
Central European computing history.

## Origins And Early Years

The club's founding in 1974 marked a pivotal moment for the burgeoning
grassroots computing scene in Brno — a scene that, at the time, existed largely
in the shadow of official state institutions. Operating under the auspices of
the ROH (Revolutionary Trade Union Movement) culture house, the club provided a
vital space where technically-minded individuals could pursue their passion for
electronics in an era when access to computing hardware was anything but
straightforward.

Membership remained modest throughout much of the club's existence, hovering
around a dozen dedicated individuals. However, the club reached its zenith in
1979, when roughly 40 members were counted on its rolls — a striking reflection
of the growing enthusiasm for microcomputing that was sweeping the evolving
landscape of Eastern European technical hobbyism.

## The Kalkul 3 Project

Without question, the club's crowning achievement was the "Kalkul 3", an
ambitious 8-bit machine completed in 1978. In a display of remarkable
resourcefulness — the kind that has come to define the era — the club's members
constructed the machine almost entirely from surplus Tesla MHB8080 components,
transforming discarded industrial parts into a genuinely functional computing
system.

Perhaps most impressively, the Kalkul 3 was not merely a curiosity or a
proof-of-concept. The city transport company put the machine to real,
practical use, employing it to verify tram timetable schedules — a task that
speaks volumes about the trust placed in these amateur engineers. Three units
were ultimately built, each one a monument to what could be accomplished with
limited means and unlimited ambition.

## Dissolution And Legacy

Sadly, the club's story came to an end in 1983, when the culture house
withdrew the room that had served as its home for nearly a decade. It was an
unceremonious conclusion to a chapter that had meant so much to so many.

Yet the club's legacy endures. Today, the club's papers — along with one
surviving Kalkul 3 board — are carefully preserved by the Moravian Technical
Museum in Brno under fond KM-14, ensuring that future generations may
appreciate this fascinating episode in the history of homebrew computing.
